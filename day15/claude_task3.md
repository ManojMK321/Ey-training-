### Haiku
**Summary:** 2-day chest pain patient discharged after stable vitals and unremarkable EKG, without troponin testing or documented risk stratification. Ibuprofen prescribed. Attending attributed limited workup to ED volume pressure.

---

## 🚩 **Critical Clinical Risk**

**Missing troponin is the dominant issue.** Two days of chest pain warrants serial troponin to rule out acute coronary syndrome (ACS), particularly since troponin-negative ACS is a real phenomenon and single troponin + normal EKG is insufficient for clearance. The note doesn't explain *why* troponin was omitted — no documented clinical reasoning, risk stratification (HEART score, TIMI), or differential diagnosis. Without that reasoning, this looks like a workup gap rather than a justified clinical decision.

Ibuprofen for undifferentiated chest pain is also questionable — NSAIDs have cardiovascular risks and shouldn't be first-line without ACS ruled out.

---

## 🚨 **Documentation/Liability Risk (Higher)**

The explicit mention of "time pressure due to ED volume" is the real exposure. This statement creates a record that the clinical decision was influenced by operational constraints rather than clinical judgment. It will read poorly in litigation as evidence that workload, not patient safety, drove the disposition. 

The attending essentially documented the *reason the workup was inadequate*, which is far worse than omitting that detail entirely.

---

## **What's Missing from the Note**

- Troponin order *or* documented reason it wasn't ordered
- Vital sign values (just "stable" is vague)
- Character/severity of pain, risk factors, associated symptoms
- Explicit risk stratification
- Specific follow-up instructions ("return if X, Y, Z symptoms" + timeframe)
- Patient safety netting discussion

---

**Bottom line:** This is a high-risk discharge that needs either re-evaluation or careful documentation cleanup. The workup itself is borderline defensible depending on the full clinical picture (age, risk profile, pain character), but attributing it to ED volume is indefensible.

### sonnet

**Summary:** A patient presenting with two days of chest pain was discharged home after stable vitals and a normal EKG, without troponin testing. Ibuprofen was prescribed. The attending documented time pressure from ED volume as context for the visit.

---

## Clinical Risks

**The absent troponin is the most serious issue.** Two days of chest pain is the kind of presentation that demands serial troponin testing to rule out ACS. A normal EKG does not exclude myocardial injury — roughly half of NSTEMI cases present with a non-diagnostic EKG. Without troponin, the workup is incomplete by most standard-of-care benchmarks, and there's no documented risk stratification (HEART score, TIMI, or equivalent) to justify the abbreviated approach.

**Ibuprofen is the wrong choice for undifferentiated chest pain.** NSAIDs carry cardiovascular risk and are relatively contraindicated until cardiac causes are excluded. Prescribing them before ruling out ACS introduces a secondary clinical concern that should be flagged.

**The follow-up instruction is vague.** "Return if symptoms worsen" sets an unclear threshold. Best practice is explicit criteria: return immediately for recurrent pain, radiation, shortness of breath, diaphoresis, or syncope.

---

## Documentation Risks

**The attending's note about time pressure is the most significant liability exposure in this record.** Documenting that ED volume influenced the clinical encounter creates a written record that operational constraints — not clinical judgment — shaped the workup. In any adverse outcome involving this patient, that sentence becomes the centerpiece of a negligence argument. It is far more damaging than simply omitting the explanation.

What's also missing from the note: vital sign values rather than just "stable," pain characterization, cardiac risk factors, differential diagnosis, any documented reasoning for skipping troponin, and a specific return precaution timeframe.

---

## Bottom Line

The clinical picture may or may not have justified a streamlined workup depending on the patient's age, risk profile, and full presentation — none of which are documented. That missing context is itself the problem. A defensible note for this discharge would need explicit risk stratification or a documented clinical rationale for the approach taken. As written, this note has both a workup gap and a self-incriminating explanation for it.

## Summary

A patient complaining of two days of chest pain was discharged home after stable vitals and an unremarkable EKG. No troponin was ordered. The plan was ibuprofen with advice to follow up if symptoms worsened. The attending documented time pressure from ED volume.

## Clinical Risks

The biggest concern is the absent troponin. Two days of chest pain is exactly the presentation where you want to rule out acute coronary syndrome, and a normal EKG alone doesn't do that — a significant share of NSTEMI cases present with a non-diagnostic EKG, so the workup as documented is incomplete by most standard-of-care benchmarks. Compounding this, there's no recorded risk stratification (a HEART score, TIMI, or even a narrative of cardiac risk factors) that would explain why a streamlined approach was clinically reasonable for this particular patient.

Ibuprofen is also a questionable choice for chest pain that hasn't been cardiac-cleared. NSAIDs carry their own cardiovascular risk and are generally something to avoid until ACS is off the table. And "follow up if symptoms worsen" is a vague safety net — good discharge instructions name specific return triggers (recurrent or radiating pain, shortness of breath, sweating, syncope) and a timeframe, rather than leaving the threshold to the patient's judgment.

## Documentation Risks

The single most exposing line in this note is the attending's mention of time pressure due to ED volume. It creates a written record suggesting that operational constraints, rather than clinical judgment, shaped the disposition. If this patient has a bad outcome, that sentence becomes the anchor of a negligence narrative — it's considerably more damaging than if the workup limitations had simply gone unexplained. Beyond that, the note is thin in ways that matter: "vitals stable" with no actual numbers, no characterization of the pain, no differential, and no documented reasoning for omitting troponin.

## Bottom Line

Whether the abbreviated workup was defensible depends entirely on details the note doesn't capture — the patient's age, risk profile, and the full character of the pain. That absence is itself the core problem. As written, this record combines a likely workup gap with a self-incriminating explanation for it, which is close to a worst-case documentation pattern. The constructive fix is for the treating clinician to revisit whether troponin or a period of observation is warranted before discharge, and to document the clinical reasoning rather than the staffing situation.
